*Sourcing note: generic to conduit finance and stated structurally. No client numbers, no named private deals, no counts, shares, medians or percentages asserted.*

- [ ] **Post draft:**

  > Most operators who believe they cannot do a bond deal are not confused. They are remembering something accurate.
  >
  > Somebody competent told them no. They have not misquoted it, and they are not being pessimistic. What almost never gets checked is a smaller thing: **which question that person was answering.**
  >
  > In conduit finance the questions sit very close together and sound identical out loud. "Can this be financed?" "Would you underwrite this?" "Would our buyers take it?" "Is this ready today?" Those are four different questions with four different answers, and only one of them is about the project.
  >
  > Three examples of the same shape:
  >
  > **"You're too small."** Often a completely correct answer to *would a public offering price well at that size?* That is a question about a distribution channel. It is not the same as *can this project be financed through a conduit at all*, because the answer to the second one turns on the fixed cost stack and the buyer, not on the size by itself.
  >
  > **"You'd need audited financials and a rating."** Frequently a correct answer to *what would an institutional buyer expect to see?* Requirements in this market follow **the buyer**, not the bond. A bank purchase, a direct placement, or a limited offering to qualified buyers is a different set of expectations than a broadly marketed rated deal. Ask which buyer the answer assumed.
  >
  > **"You're two years away."** Usually a correct answer about *the timeline the speaker had in view* — a site, a permit, an entitlement, a certificate. It is rarely an answer about the readiness and disclosure work, which mostly runs in parallel rather than in sequence. Two years to a permit does not mean two years until you should start.
  >
  > None of those people were wrong. They answered what they were asked, from where they sat, honestly and for free. The failure is structural: **the answer travels, and the question doesn't.** A year later the operator is carrying "we can't do that" with no memory of who said it, what they were asked, or what they were assuming.
  >
  > So here is the exercise, and it costs nothing:
  >
  > **1. Write down the no you are carrying, in the exact words you remember.**
  >
  > **2. Name who said it.** Their seat tells you which question they were built to answer. A banker is answering about distribution. A lender is answering about credit. A CPA is answering about accounting. A peer is answering about their own deal, in their own state, in their own year.
  >
  > **3. Write the question you now think they were actually answering.**
  >
  > **4. Ask whether anyone has ever answered *your* question** — can this specific project, at this size, with this asset life, be financed through a conduit, and what would have to be true.
  >
  > Most of the time, step 4 comes back blank. Not "no." Blank. Nobody ever ran that one.
  >
  > That gap is the quiet reason operators disappear from this market without ever being turned down. There is no rejection to point at, no filing, no file. There is a remembered sentence, answering a question that was never theirs.
  >
  > *(We run the operator-side readiness and disclosure system on our own live engagements. The above is generic to conduit finance; programs, buyer expectations, fee structures and issuer processes vary considerably by state and by issuer, and reflect no client's file. Confirm your own situation with bond counsel and a municipal advisor before acting on any of it.)*
