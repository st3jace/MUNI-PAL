## Content — 1 proof-led post (LinkedIn)

**Angle 3 · Hidden-cost spotlight · FRESH cut — the substitution tax: what you finance with while you wait doesn't go away when the bond arrives.** *(Longest-unused usable angle; last ran 07-20. Distinct from every prior angle-3 cut: 06-22 document-churn, 06-29 board-calendar-slip, 07-06 premature-no, 07-13 carry-clock, 07-20 readiness-shelf-life/re-underwrite tax. Those all price **delay**. This one prices the opposite behavior — the operator who does **not** wait, takes interim capital, and permanently bakes its terms into the project. Honest positioning, composite, no client numbers, proof library still empty.)*

- [ ] **Post draft:**
  > When a facility project's bond financing stalls, almost nobody actually stops. That's the part the "cost of delay" conversation misses.
  >
  > The project keeps moving, and it gets funded by whatever is available in the meantime: a bridge loan, a bank facility at a shorter tenor, an equity slice sold to close a gap, a seller carry, a phase built with cash that was meant for phase two.
  >
  > None of that is a mistake. It's what competent operators do when a building has to get built.
  >
  > But here's the part that gets underestimated: **the interim structure usually doesn't disappear when the bond finally shows up. It becomes a permanent feature of the deal.**
  >
  > A few ways that happens in practice:
  >
  > **The equity you sold is sold.** The percentage you gave up to cover a 9-month gap doesn't come back at closing. You bought time with ownership, permanently, at the worst possible negotiating moment.
  >
  > **The interim lender is now a party to your bond deal.** They have a lien, a payoff calculation, an intercreditor position, and an opinion about timing. You added a seat at the table, and that seat has to be satisfied before you close.
  >
  > **Covenants outlive the loan.** Reporting requirements, cash sweeps, and restrictions negotiated under pressure often carry into the takeout because they've become the credit story everyone underwrites you against.
  >
  > **The phasing you chose under constraint is now the building.** Phase one built at higher unit cost, with a design compromise made to fit the cash you had, is not re-decidable later.
  >
  > So the honest way to price a stalled bond deal isn't the carry cost of waiting. It's this: **whatever capital you accept while you wait, price it as if you'll live with it for the life of the asset — because you usually will.**
  >
  > Which changes what "getting ready early" is worth. Readiness isn't mainly about being faster. It's about not being the party with the least leverage in the room when a gap has to get filled *right now*.
  >
  > The cheapest version of this is boring: have the entity documents, the ownership chart, the consistent financials, and the governance authority sitting in one place **before** the timeline gets tight — so that when a gap opens, you're choosing your interim capital instead of accepting it.
  >
  > *(This is the readiness system we run on our own live engagements. Pattern above is composite, from public conduit deal structures — no client specifics.)*
