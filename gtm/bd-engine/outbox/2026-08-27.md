- [ ] **Post draft:**

  > Most warnings about being underprepared for a bond financing describe a slow deal. Delays, re-trades, a missed board cycle, a "no" that arrives late.
  >
  > **The version worth worrying about is the one where nothing goes wrong.**
  >
  > The deal closes. Roughly on schedule. Everybody is professional, nobody is angry, and the operator walks away believing that preparation would have bought them a few weeks at most.
  >
  > **Here is what I think actually happened.** Uncertainty does not disappear because a deal closes. It gets priced. When the people underwriting a project cannot get clean answers — about the operating history, about who is answerable for what, about how the numbers were produced — they do not usually walk. They protect themselves in the structure instead. That is their job, and it is a reasonable thing to do.
  >
  > **Protection lives in terms.** More conservative sizing. Reserves and cushions set where they cover the unknown rather than the known. Covenants written tighter, and tested more often, because tighter tests substitute for confidence somebody could not get any other way. Conditions and consents that keep a hand on the wheel. Sometimes a credit enhancement or a structural feature that carries its own ongoing cost. The specific mechanisms vary enormously by sector, structure, credit and market conditions — I am describing a direction, not a formula.
  >
  > **Two things make this bill invisible.**
  >
  > **It never arrives as a bill.** Nobody sends a line item that says "this is what your disorganisation cost." It arrives as a term sheet that looks like the market, because it *is* the market — for a borrower who presented the way you presented.
  >
  > **You never see the alternative.** There is exactly one version of your deal, and you got it. The version that a fully legible borrower would have been offered does not exist anywhere you can look at it. This is the whole reason the cost is so easy to under-rate: every other cost of unreadiness eventually shows up on a calendar or an invoice, and this one has nowhere to show up at all.
  >
  > **And unlike delay, it does not end at closing.** A few lost weeks are gone once. A tighter covenant is somebody's job every reporting period, for as long as the bonds are outstanding. A structural cushion sits in the deal for its whole life. That is the part that makes this worth more attention than it gets: it is the only cost of unreadiness that compounds by simply continuing to exist.
  >
  > **What to do with this, free:** before you are negotiating anything, work out which questions about your project a stranger cannot currently answer from documents you already have. Not questions you can answer in a meeting — questions the file answers without you in the room. Every one of those is a place where somebody else's caution will do the answering for you, and caution is expensive in a way that does not look expensive at the time.
  >
  > **The goal is not to look good.** The goal is to be legible, early, so that the terms you are offered reflect your project rather than the uncertainty around it.
  >
  > *(We run the operator-side readiness and disclosure system on our own live engagements. The above is generic to conduit finance and reflects no client's file; pricing, structure and covenant practice vary considerably by sector, credit, issuer and market. Nothing here is legal, tax or financial advice — confirm your own situation with bond counsel and a municipal advisor.)*
