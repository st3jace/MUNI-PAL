- [ ] **Post draft:**

  > Every walkthrough of a bond deal you will ever read is organized the same way: **in order.** Step one, step two, step three, closing.
  >
  > That ordering tells you what happens. It tells you almost nothing about where your work goes.
  >
  > Try sorting the same deal a different way. **Not by when each piece happens — by how many times each piece gets made.**
  >
  > Some things in a deal are produced **exactly once.** A resolution is adopted. A closing happens. A document is executed. These are the parts that feel like the deal, they are the parts that go on the calendar, and once they are done they are done.
  >
  > Other things are produced **over and over.** The description of the project. The sources and uses. The financial projections. The answer to "what exactly does this entity own and owe." Each of these gets built, then rebuilt when an assumption moves, then rebuilt when a new party joins and asks from scratch, then rebuilt when a number upstream changes, then rebuilt for a different audience who needs it in a different shape.
  >
  > **Nobody plans for the second category, because a step list has no column for "again."**
  >
  > This is why deals so often feel heavier than the timeline implied. The timeline was not wrong about the sequence. It was silent about the repetition — and the repetition is where the hours actually went.
  >
  > **The useful part is that the second category is knowable in advance.** A piece of your deal will get re-cut many times if it has any of these properties:
  >
  > **It depends on an assumption that is not yet fixed.** Anything downstream of a number still being negotiated will be rebuilt every time that number moves.
  > **More than one party needs it, in different forms.** The same underlying facts, wanted as a narrative by one reader and as a schedule by another, is not one artifact — it is one set of facts and several renderings.
  > **It is the thing every new participant asks for first.** Each party that joins arrives with no context and starts at the beginning.
  >
  > **And that changes what "being prepared" means.** Preparation is usually imagined as *having documents*. Sorted this way, it is obviously something else: it is **having the high-repetition pieces built so that re-cutting them is cheap.** One place the underlying facts live, so a changed assumption updates one source instead of six documents. A project description that can be rendered long or short without being rewritten. Figures that trace back to something, so that "where did this come from" is a lookup and not an excavation.
  >
  > A document built to be produced once and a document built to be produced eleven times look identical on the day they are first delivered. They stop looking identical the third time somebody asks.
  >
  > **A free exercise, and you can run it on a deal you already did.** Open the folder. For each significant artifact, count the versions. Not "was this hard" — **how many times did we make this.** The two or three items at the top of that list are where your next deal's effort is going to go, and they are the only items where building for reuse pays you back.
  >
  > Most operators discover that list in retrospect, once, and then do not use it. It is the cheapest piece of intelligence a completed deal leaves behind.
  >
