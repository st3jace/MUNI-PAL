- [ ] **Post draft:**

  > Every operator I talk to can tell me what an unready deal cost them. The extra months. The professional fees. The people pulled off the business to chase documents.
  >
  > Almost none of them can tell me what it cost the other people at the table. And that is the bill that decides whether there is a second deal.
  >
  > A conduit financing runs on a small crowd of people who are not on your payroll: bond counsel, the issuer's staff, a municipal advisor, a lender's credit team, an underwriter, a trustee, a board that meets on a fixed calendar. When a borrower shows up unready, the work does not disappear. It moves.
  >
  > **It moves onto counsel**, as hours that get written down rather than billed, because nobody wants to send a first-time borrower an invoice for the education.
  >
  > **It moves onto the issuer's staff**, who re-explain the same requirement three times and reschedule an item that was supposed to be decided in one sitting.
  >
  > **It moves onto the board**, whose agenda has a fixed number of slots and now has one fewer.
  >
  > **It moves onto whoever vouched for you** — the advisor or the intermediary who said "this one is real." That person spent something they cannot get back cheaply.
  >
  > None of that shows up in your cost of issuance. There is no line item, no statement, and no moment where anyone tells you the balance. Which is exactly why it goes unmanaged: you cannot economize on a cost you were never shown.
  >
  > But the people who absorbed it remember. Not resentfully — professionally. It becomes a quiet prior about how much friction your name carries. And priors like that are what determine:
  >
  > - who gets a call back in a week instead of a month
  > - whose item gets slotted onto a crowded agenda and whose waits for the next meeting
  > - who gets told "that will not work, try this instead" early, and who gets told nothing at all until it is late
  > - which borrower an advisor puts forward when someone upstream asks if there is anything worth looking at
  >
  > Here is the part that reframes the whole thing. **Readiness is usually sold as a way to make your own deal faster. Its larger return is that it is the cheapest possible thing to be, from the point of view of everyone whose help you will need again.**
  >
  > Two questions worth sitting with, and neither one takes a consultant:
  >
  > **1. On your last financing — or your last serious attempt — who did work that never appeared on any invoice?** Name the actual people. That list is your real balance sheet with this market.
  >
  > **2. If that same group were asked tomorrow whether they would take your next deal, what would they say before they said yes?** The hesitation is the number.
  >
  > The reason this matters more for small and first-time borrowers than for large repeat ones is uncomfortable but simple: a large issuer's relationships survive a rough transaction because there is volume behind them. A first-time operator's relationships are the whole account. Spend it once and there is nothing behind it.
  >
  > None of which is an argument for being intimidated. It is an argument for the opposite — arrive with the file already assembled, and you are not just faster, you are *cheap to work with*. In a market that runs on a small number of repeat professionals, being cheap to work with compounds in a way that being large does not.
  >
  > *(We run the operator-side readiness and disclosure system on our own live engagements. The above is generic to conduit finance; practice, fee structures and issuer processes vary considerably by state and by issuer, and reflect no client's file. Confirm your own situation with bond counsel and a municipal advisor.)*
