- [ ] **Post draft:**

  > If you are trying to read the conduit market, you are probably being told to go read official statements. That is good advice and it is slow, and most operators bounce off it.
  >
  > There is a free step before it that almost nobody takes: **read only the titles.**
  >
  > Every publicly issued deal has a full formal name, and it is visible without opening a single document. It looks like boilerplate. It is not. **A deal's title is a compressed record of what the market required of that borrower before it would fund the project.**
  >
  > Here is what is actually sitting in those names.
  >
  > **The issuer.** Somebody lent their access to this borrower. That name tells you a route existed for a project like theirs — which is the single most useful fact for an operator who has been told no route exists.
  >
  > **A series letter — 2024A, 2024B.** The deal was split into pieces. Something about it needed separating, and the borrower had to satisfy whatever drove that split.
  >
  > **The word "Taxable" sitting beside a tax-exempt series.** Part of what was being financed did not fit inside the tax-exempt category, or someone needed it kept apart. Either way it is a statement about the project's composition, not just its pricing.
  >
  > **"Senior" and "Subordinate."** The capital stack was tiered. Somebody had to be paid before somebody else, and the deal was structured so that could be documented.
  >
  > **"Refunding."** This deal is about debt that already existed, not a new facility. Deals like that answer a different question, and reading them as construction comparables will mislead you.
  >
  > **The security phrase in the long name** — revenue bonds, mortgage revenue bonds, solid waste disposal revenue bonds, and so on. That phrase names what is actually pledged and, often, which category the deal had to qualify under. It is the closest thing to a plain-language statement of what the borrower had to prove.
  >
  > **None of these words describe the project.** They describe what had to be added to the project before the market would take the paper. That is precisely the thing an operator cannot see from the outside — and it is printed on the outside.
  >
  > **The exercise, and it costs nothing but an afternoon of attention.**
  >
  > Pull five completed deals that resemble yours — same sector, roughly comparable purpose. Write down **only their full formal titles.** Do not open the documents yet.
  >
  > Then underline every word in each title that is not the borrower's name, the year, or the word "bonds."
  >
  > What is left is the market's own vocabulary for deals like yours. Some words will appear in every title. Some will appear in a few. Some will appear once.
  >
  > **Now take that underlined list to your bond counsel and municipal advisor and ask one question about each item: would my deal have this, and if so, what does it require of me?**
  >
  > That is a much better meeting than "how do we get bond-ready." You are no longer asking them to describe an entire process from scratch. You are handing them a short list drawn from real deals and asking which lines apply to you. **It also tells you which official statements are worth the hours** — the ones whose titles matched yours most closely.
  >
  > Two honest limits, because this method has them. **A title is a summary, not the deal** — the reasons behind every one of those words live in the documents, and only the documents are authoritative. And **the words do not carry a fixed meaning across sectors, structures, issuers, states or years**; do not assume a term means in your deal what it meant in someone else's. The titles are a map of what to ask about. They are not the answer.
  >
  > *(We run the operator-side readiness and disclosure system on our own live engagements. The above is generic to conduit finance and reflects no client's file — the exercise deliberately uses deals you select yourself. Nothing here is legal, tax or financial advice; what any structural feature would require of your project is a question for bond counsel and a municipal advisor.)*
