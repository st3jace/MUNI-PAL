**Angle 6 (market signal) — fresh cut: "the floor is lower than you were told."** Longest-unused usable angle; last ran 08-10. Angle 4 (proof story) remains blocked by the empty proof library.

*Prior angle-6 cuts, for the record — today is organized on a different axis from all of them: 06-19 / 06-26 / 07-03 / 07-10 sector diversity · 07-17 geography of borrowers · 07-29 who issues (consolidation of conduit platforms) · 08-10 who comes back (repeat issuers). Today's axis is **how big they are** — the size distribution, and specifically its bottom end, which is the number operators are most often wrong about.*

*Sourcing note: the public record is cited **qualitatively only**. No counts, shares, medians, or percentages are asserted, because the housing-corpus sample on hand is too small to support one and has not refreshed since 07-18. Nothing below depends on a number the engine cannot stand behind.*

- [ ] **Post draft:**

  > Ask a facility operator why they have never looked at tax-exempt bond financing and you will usually get a number back. "We're too small." "That starts at a hundred million." "That's for hospitals and universities."
  >
  > The number is almost always wrong, and it is interesting where it comes from. Nobody read it in a statute. It arrives second-hand — from a banker describing the deals *they* like to work on, from a peer who tried once and gave up, from a general sense that public finance is for institutions. It hardens into a fact without ever being checked.
  >
  > The public record is right there and it disagrees. Conduit issues get done for single-facility operators, for one-property partnerships formed for one project, for borrowers with no prior market history and no rating. Small industrial development boards and housing authorities in places you have never heard of issue for borrowers the size of a regional business. You can go read the filings yourself — the borrower's own name is on them.
  >
  > So the floor is not where most operators think it is. But here is the part that matters more, because it explains why the wrong number survives:
  >
  > **The binding constraint is usually not par. It is the fixed cost stack sitting on top of par.**
  >
  > Counsel, issuer fees, the official statement, trustee, rating or credit enhancement if you use it — a meaningful share of that cost barely moves with deal size. The same work happens on a small deal as on a large one. So the question "are we big enough" is really "is the fixed cost of getting there a tolerable percentage of what we are raising, over the life of what we are building." That is arithmetic about your specific project, not a threshold someone else can quote you.
  >
  > Which means the honest answer to "are we too small" is: **it depends on numbers you can actually get, and nobody has run them for you.**
  >
  > Three things that move that arithmetic more than size does, and which operators routinely undervalue:
  >
  > **Whether the project has a long life.** Fixed issuance costs spread over thirty years read very differently from the same costs spread over seven. A building tilts the maths in a way equipment usually does not.
  >
  > **Whether there will be a second deal.** Much of the first deal's cost is learning — building the entity file, the disclosure posture, the relationships. If a second issue is plausible, the first one is buying an asset you reuse. If it genuinely is one-and-done, be honest about that; it changes the answer.
  >
  > **Whether you are ready, or six months from ready.** This is the one that actually kills small deals. Not the par amount — the professional hours burned assembling a file that should have already existed. Cost overruns on small transactions are usually *readiness* overruns wearing a different name.
  >
  > None of that requires a banker to tell you. It requires an hour with your own project.
  >
  > The failure mode I would flag is not operators trying and being turned down. It is operators **self-selecting out** against a number they were handed, never testing it, and never appearing anywhere — not in a rejection, not in a filing, not in anyone's pipeline. That is an invisible failure. There is no record of the deal that was never explored.
  >
  > If you have been carrying a threshold in your head, the useful exercise is to find out where it came from. If the answer is "someone told me once," it is worth an afternoon to check.
  >
  > *(We run the operator-side readiness and disclosure system on our own live engagements. The above is generic to conduit finance; thresholds, fees and issuer practice vary considerably by state and by issuer, and reflect no client's file. Confirm your own arithmetic with bond counsel and a municipal advisor before acting on it.)*
