## Content — 1 post

**Angle 1 (deal anatomy) — fresh cut: "run the deal backwards from the closing date."** Longest-unused usable angle; last ran 08-06. Angle 4 (proof story) remains blocked by the empty proof library.

*Prior angle-1 cuts, for the record — this one is organized differently from all of them: 06-17 & 06-24 stage-by-stage walk · 07-01 stage-list · 07-08 time-distribution across the four legs · 07-15 the diligence-list inflection point · 07-27 the cast list of parties · 08-06 the one-way doors (cost-of-reversal). Every one of those runs the anatomy **forward**. This one runs it in **retrograde** — start at the closing table and ask what had to already be true a week before, a month before, a quarter before — because that is the only version of the anatomy that converts a target date into a start date.*

- [ ] **Post draft:**

  > Most explanations of a bond financing start at the beginning. That's the wrong direction if you have a date in mind. Run it backwards instead — from the closing table — and it tells you something a forward walkthrough never does.
  >
  > **Closing day.** Documents get signed and proceeds get funded — usually into an account controlled by a trustee under an indenture, released against requisitions, not wired to your operating account. Worth internalizing early: the day the money exists is not the day you can spend it however you like.
  >
  > **Back up a couple of weeks.** Pricing and the final official statement. Whatever your organization can say about itself is now fixed in a public document. Nothing gets fixed here that wasn't already true weeks earlier.
  >
  > **Back up a month or two.** The preliminary official statement goes out. This is the real deadline for your financial statements, your ownership structure, your project description, your litigation and insurance disclosure. Not "close to final" — final enough that a market reads it.
  >
  > **Back up another month or so.** The public approval step and the issuer's authorizing action — hearing notice periods, a board or council that meets on its own schedule, a project described in terms you then have to live with. Note what this is: someone else's calendar. You cannot compress it by working harder.
  >
  > **Back up further still.** The issuer's inducement or official intent action — the step that sets the boundary for which already-incurred costs can be reimbursed from proceeds later. It is the earliest step in the deal and one of the few with direct financial consequence, and it is routinely skipped because at that moment it feels premature. (Confirm the specifics with bond counsel — this is one of the places where being early is worth real money.)
  >
  > **And behind all of it,** the part with no deadline attached: entity documents, a clean ownership chart, financials on a consistent basis across a consistent set of entities, site control with a term that outlives the financing, board authority recorded with signer and limit.
  >
  > Here is what the backwards read exposes. The last sixty to ninety days of a financing are almost entirely other people's calendars — notice periods, meeting dates, review cycles, printing. Those are effectively fixed. The only genuinely compressible part of the whole timeline is the front — and the front is precisely the part with no external deadline, no one chasing you, and therefore no forcing function.
  >
  > So when an operator says "we want to close by Q2," the useful response isn't a feasibility opinion. It's a subtraction problem. Work backwards from the date through the fixed steps, and you arrive at a start date. Most of the time that start date is in the past — which is not bad news. It's the first piece of actionable information anyone has given them.
  >
  > *(We run the operator-side readiness and disclosure system on our own live engagements. The sequence above is generic to conduit finance; specifics vary by issuer and by state, and reflect no client's file.)*

**Honest-positioning check:** no client numbers, no invented figures, no named private deals, no asserted durations presented as fixed rules — the sequence is described generically with an explicit "varies by issuer / confirm with bond counsel" qualifier.
