*Prior angle-1 cuts, for the record — today is organized on an axis none of them used: 06-17 & 06-24 stage-by-stage walk · 07-01 stage-list · 07-08 time-distribution across the legs · 07-15 the diligence-list inflection point · 07-27 the cast list of parties · 08-06 the one-way doors (cost-of-reversal) · 08-13 retrograde from the closing date. *Sourcing note: generic to conduit finance and stated structurally. No client numbers, no named private deals, no counts, shares, medians or percentages asserted.*

- [ ] **Post draft:**

  > Ask an operator mid-deal where things stand and you usually get a mood. "We're getting close." "It's with the lawyers." "Waiting on the issuer."
  >
  > None of those are a status. Here is one that is:
  >
  > **At any given moment, a conduit deal is exactly one document, being marked up by exactly one person.**
  >
  > Not a stage. Not a phase. A document. Somebody has the pen. If you can name the document and name the person, you know where your deal is. If you cannot, your deal is not slow — **it is parked**, and nobody in the room has told you, because from their seat nothing is wrong.
  >
  > The anatomy of a deal reads very differently when you tell it this way. Instead of a sequence of stages, it is a relay: a document is created, it is worked, it is handed on, and while it is on one desk it is generally not on any other. The application. The inducement or authorizing resolution. The term sheet. The diligence request list. The financing documents. The disclosure document. The closing certificates. Roughly speaking, one of those is live at a time, and the live one *is* the deal.
  >
  > Three things follow, and they are the useful part:
  >
  > **1. "It's with the lawyers" is not a status, it is a missing name.** Which lawyer, on which document, and what are they waiting for from whom? Counsel is frequently waiting on an input from the borrower while the borrower believes they are waiting on counsel. That is not anyone behaving badly. It is the predictable result of nobody naming the document out loud.
  >
  > **2. Handoffs are where time is lost, not desks.** Work happening on a document is usually fast. The gap between one person finishing and the next person starting is where the calendar goes — and it is invisible, because no one is late for anything. Nothing is overdue when nothing was named.
  >
  > **3. There is exactly one document you always control.** Every artifact above sits on somebody else's desk for most of its life. Your readiness and disclosure file does not. It is the only document in the deal that you can advance on a day when the pen is with someone else — and it is the one that determines how fast every other document can be produced when your turn comes.
  >
  > That is why "we're waiting on them" is such an expensive sentence. It is usually true. It is also usually not the whole day.
  >
  > So the exercise, which takes about a minute:
  >
  > **Name the document your deal currently is. Name the person holding the pen. Name what they are waiting on.**
  >
  > If you can answer all three, you are in a deal that is moving and you can stop guessing. If you cannot answer them, the next email is not a nudge — it is "which document are we on and whose turn is it?" That question is not pushy. In my experience it is the one everyone at the table is quietly relieved someone finally asked.
  >
  > And on the day the pen is genuinely not yours: the readiness file is still yours. Work that.
  >
  > *(We run the operator-side readiness and disclosure system on our own live engagements. The above is generic to conduit finance; document sets, sequencing, issuer processes and counsel practice vary considerably by state and by issuer, and reflect no client's file. Confirm your own situation with bond counsel and a municipal advisor before acting on any of it.)*
