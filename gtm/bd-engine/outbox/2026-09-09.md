**Fresh cut: "half your deal is not about the project."**

*Organizing axis: **subject matter.*** Every prior angle-1 cut sorts the deal by something structural — 06-17 and 06-24 by stage, 07-01 by stage list, 07-08 by where time goes, 07-15 by the diligence inflection point, 07-27 by the cast of parties, 08-06 by reversibility, 08-13 by working backwards from the closing date, 08-24 by which single artifact the deal currently *is*, 09-01 by how many times each piece gets redone. **None of them sorts by what each piece is actually about.** Today does: there are two files, the project file and the entity file, and operators arrive having built one of them.

- [ ] **Post draft:**

> **Half your deal is not about the project.**
>
> When an operator gets ready for a bond, they get the project ready. Site. Cost. What it will earn. Who will use it. That work is real and it is necessary, and it is roughly half of what has to exist.
>
> The other half is not about the project at all. It is about you.
>
> Who is the borrower, exactly — which entity, formed where, owned by whom? Who has authority to sign, and what document says so? What does that entity already own and already owe? Who has run something like this before, and what happened? If there are related entities, how do they connect, and which of them is on the hook?
>
> Two files. The project file answers *is this thing worth building.* The entity file answers *can this borrower be lent to.* They are prepared by different people, from different records, and they fail for different reasons.
>
> Here is why this is worth naming. **Preparation effort follows attention, and attention follows enthusiasm.** Operators are enthusiastic about the project — it is the thing they want to build. Almost nobody is enthusiastic about reconstructing an ownership chart or locating the resolution that granted signing authority. So the project file gets built early and gets built well, and the entity file gets built late, under pressure, from memory, by whoever is free.
>
> Which produces the pattern I keep meeting: an operator who is genuinely well-prepared, stalled, and confused about why — because the half they prepared is not the half that is holding things up.
>
> Three things follow.
>
> **The entity file is mostly retrieval, not creation.** The documents largely already exist. They are in a drawer, in an old counsel's files, in a former CFO's inbox. That makes it a much smaller job than it feels like — and it is also why it can be started today, with no project decisions made and nothing else settled.
>
> **It is the half that does not move when the project does.** Change the scope, the site, the size, and the project file gets re-cut. The entity file mostly does not care. It is the most durable preparation available, which is a strange thing to leave for last.
>
> **A gap here does not read like a gap.** A missing cost estimate reads as *not finished yet.* A missing answer about who owns the borrower and who may sign for it reads as something else entirely, to people whose job is to be careful.
>
> **The exercise, and it takes an afternoon:** write down the exact legal name of the entity that would be the borrower. Then name the document that says who can sign for it, and where that document physically is. If either answer is "I'd have to check," you have found work that is entirely within your control and does not depend on a single decision anyone else has to make.
>
> *This is a pattern from operating experience, not a rule. What each file must contain varies by sector, structure, state and issuer, and eligibility for any particular financing is a question for bond counsel and a municipal advisor on your actual facts — not for a post.*

*No counts, shares, medians, percentages, basis points, fee figures, client numbers, timelines or named private deals asserted. No claim about market conditions or about what any party "usually" does. The central claim is verifiable by the reader against their own records, which is exactly the instruction given.
