## Content — 1 proof-led post (LinkedIn)

**Angle 3 · Hidden-cost spotlight · FRESH cut — "the deal is staffed out of your operating bench": the cost that never shows up in the financing, because it shows up in the business.** *(Longest-unused usable angle; last ran 07-30. The axis today is **who actually does the work and what stops while they do it** — internal capacity, not money or time. Structurally distinct from every prior angle-3 cut: 06-22 document-request churn (volume of back-and-forth), 06-29 board-calendar slip, 07-06 the premature "no", 07-13 carry clock, 07-20 readiness shelf-life / re-buying diligence, 07-30 substitution tax / interim capital. Those price delay, friction, or capital. This one prices **attention** — and unlike the others the meter runs on the operating side of the house, where nobody is looking for it. Honest positioning, composite, no client numbers, proof library still empty.)*

- [ ] **Post — draft:**

> When operators price a bond financing, they price the visible things: counsel, advisor, issuer fees, cost of issuance, the rate.

> The largest cost is usually none of those. **It's that the deal gets staffed out of your operating bench.**

> Think about who actually assembles a financing file at a mid-sized operator. The controller, because they're the only one who can produce financials on a consistent basis. The facilities lead, because they hold the permits, the surveys, and the maintenance history. The COO or ED, because signature authority and board minutes run through them. And almost always one long-tenured person who simply *knows where everything is* — and whose real job title has nothing to do with finance.

> Those are not spare people. They are, by definition, your best generalists — which is exactly why they get handed the deal.

> So for six, nine, twelve months, a fraction of your operating capacity quietly moves onto the financing. And the cost lands somewhere it will never be attributed:

> — month-end close slips a few days, then a few more
> — a vendor renewal gets rubber-stamped instead of negotiated
> — a hiring cycle stretches because the person who runs interviews is chasing signatures
> — preventive maintenance turns into a deferred list
> — a grant or filing deadline gets met at the minimum instead of properly

> None of that appears on a sources-and-uses. It appears in next year's numbers, disconnected from the thing that caused it.

> And here's the part that makes it compound: operators describe this afterward as **"the deal took nine months."** That's not quite what happened. What happened is that the business ran a key person short for nine months, and the deal is simply what that person was doing instead.

> The fix isn't heroic, but it does have to happen *before* you start, not in month three when everyone is already underwater. Two decisions:

> **1. Name the seat you're renting out — and name what it stops doing.** If your controller is going to lose 30% of their capacity for two quarters, decide now what gets dropped or backfilled. An unnamed cost gets paid anyway; it just gets paid out of whatever was weakest that week.

> **2. Decide what you're buying versus absorbing.** Some of this work genuinely requires your people — nobody else knows your operation. A large share of it doesn't: assembling the file, maintaining it, tracking the request list, keeping the record current between deals. That part is a seat you can rent instead of drafting one of your five best people into it.

> The operators who come through a financing without a hangover generally aren't the ones with the cleanest projects. They're the ones who decided in advance which of their own people were *not* going to be spent on it.

> *(This is the operator-side readiness-and-disclosure work we run on our own live engagements — the seat, not the advice.)*
