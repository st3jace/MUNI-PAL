## Content — 1 post

**Angle 5 (checklist excerpt) — fresh cut: "the readiness checklist is a reconciliation, not a collection — the items have to agree with each other."** Longest-unused usable angle; last ran 08-07. Angle 4 (proof story) remains blocked by the empty proof library.

*Prior angle-5 cuts, for the record — this one is organized on a different axis from all of them: 06-25 what the lender checks · 07-02 the questions a lender asks · 07-09 the pre-check · 07-16 who owns each line · 07-28 prerequisite-independence (what you can finish before you have a project) · 08-07 form vs. substance (items you have, in an unusable form). Every one of those evaluates each item **individually** — present/absent, whose job, what shape. Today's axis is **cross-document agreement**: each item can be individually perfect and the file still fails, because the same fact appears in five documents produced by five different people and no two of them say it identically.*

- [ ] **Post draft:**

  > A readiness checklist looks like a shopping list. Have the financials, have site control, have the resolution, have the permits. Tick them off, you're ready.
  >
  > That's not what diligence does with it. Diligence reads the documents **against each other**. And the failure I see most isn't a missing item — it's five present items that don't quite agree.
  >
  > Four facts that appear in almost every document in the file, and almost never identically:
  >
  > **The legal entity.** The name on the deed, the name in the operating agreement, the name on the tax return, the name on the permit, and the name of the entity actually proposing to borrow. Usually four of the five match. The mismatch is rarely fraud and almost always history — a holding company formed later, an LLC converted to an LP, a d/b/a that stuck, a property still held where it was parked at acquisition. Each document is correct. The set isn't consistent.
  >
  > **The size of the project.** Unit count, bed count, square footage, tons per day, capacity. It appears in the appraisal, the plans, the pro forma, and whatever you submitted to the issuer. Design changes are normal. Design changes that reached the drawings but not the pro forma are the ones that show up as a question you can't answer in the room.
  >
  > **The dollar total.** Total project cost in the budget, in the sources and uses, and in the amount the board authorized. Worth checking separately: which costs you incurred *before* the issuer's inducement or official-intent action, because that boundary determines what can be reimbursed from proceeds at all. A budget that quietly assumes otherwise is a document that disagrees with the record. (One for bond counsel, early.)
  >
  > **The signer.** The resolution authorizes a person, or a title, up to an amount, for a period. Then time passes — someone's title changes, the number moves, the authorization ages. The document is real. What it authorizes and what you're now asking someone to sign have drifted apart.
  >
  > Here's why this particular failure is so common: nobody owns it. The accountant owns the financials, the architect owns the drawings, counsel owns the entity documents, the broker owns the pro forma. Each is right within their own lane, and each was produced at a different time for a different reason. **The first person who reads all of them side by side is usually a stranger doing diligence, at speed, specifically looking for exactly this.** That is a bad first reader to lose a week to.
  >
  > The check is cheap and you can run it this month without hiring anyone. Put your five most important documents on one table and trace those four facts through all of them — the legal name, the size, the total, the signer. Anything that doesn't tie, write it down. You now have either a short list of clerical fixes or a real finding you'd rather have in a quiet week than in a diligence call.
  >
  > Most of what comes off that exercise is an afternoon's work. The value isn't the afternoon — it's that you found it, on your schedule, instead of someone else finding it on theirs.
  >
  > *(We run the operator-side readiness and disclosure system on our own live engagements. The above is generic to conduit finance; specifics vary by issuer and by state, and reflect no client's file.)*

**Honest-positioning check:** no client numbers, no invented figures, no named private deals, no claimed frequencies presented as data ("the failure I see most" is stated as an observation, not a statistic). The reimbursement point carries an explicit confirm-with-counsel qualifier.
